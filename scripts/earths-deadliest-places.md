# EARTH'S DEADLIEST PLACES: Where Nature Was Never Meant to Be Visited

*Earth Uncharted — narration script. Lines starting with `##` are segment labels, not narration.*

## COLD OPEN

On every continent, there are places that do not want us there.

A lake so caustic it turns animals to stone.

An island where every step could land on a viper.

A river hot enough to cook anything that falls in.

And a mountain whose lava runs faster than a car on a highway.

Some kill slowly. Others kill in seconds.

Yet people keep coming. To study them. To cross them. Even to live beside them.

This is Earth at its most lethal.

## 1. LAKE NATRON — Tanzania

In northern Tanzania, close to the Kenyan border, a lake lies flat and red beneath an active volcano.

From the air, it looks like a wound in the savanna. Crimson water. White crust. Pink shallows fading into rust.

Lake Natron.

The color comes from microorganisms. Salt-loving bacteria that thrive where almost nothing else can.

Because this water is not normal water.

Volcanic ash from Ol Doinyo Lengai, the mountain to the south, has loaded the lake with sodium carbonate. In places, the water is as caustic as household ammonia. In the shallows, it can grow hotter than a bath.

Skin burns. Eyes sting. Fish survive only where hot springs dilute the edges.

And the minerals do something stranger.

Birds and bats that die in the lake don't rot. They calcify. The same compound ancient Egyptians used to preserve their dead coats the bodies, sealing them in a pale chalky shell.

Feathers intact. Wings still spread. Frozen in the exact pose they fell in.

But the lake does not only take life.

Every breeding season, more than two million lesser flamingos arrive here. Most of the species' entire population, crowding onto islands of salt.

They come for the danger. The caustic water keeps predators away. No jackal will cross it. No hyena will wade in.

So the flamingos raise their chicks in a place that would burn anything else alive.

A lake that kills. And a lake that protects.

On Lake Natron, they are the same thing.

## 2. LAKE NYOS — Cameroon

Two thousand kilometers to the west, another lake looks completely harmless.

High in the volcanic hills of Cameroon, Lake Nyos sits blue and still, ringed by green slopes and quiet farmland.

No bubbling. No smell. No warning.

On the evening of August 21st, 1986, the lake exhaled.

For centuries, carbon dioxide had been seeping up from magma deep below, dissolving into the cold bottom water. The lake held it the way a sealed bottle holds fizz.

Then something disturbed the layers. Possibly a landslide. Possibly a shift in temperature.

The gas came out all at once.

A cloud of carbon dioxide burst from the surface, heavier than air, and rolled down into the valleys. It moved silently. It hugged the ground. It flowed into villages up to twenty-five kilometers away.

People went to sleep and never woke up. Cattle collapsed in the fields. Birds and insects vanished.

By morning, more than seventeen hundred people were dead.

There had been no fire. No explosion. Just air that could no longer be breathed.

Today, pipes rise from the middle of Lake Nyos, venting gas from the deep water in slow, steady fountains.

The pressure is being released one breath at a time.

Because the volcano beneath it has not stopped feeding the lake.

## 3. LAKE KIVU — Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo

But Nyos is small.

On the border between Rwanda and the Congo, Lake Kivu stretches for nearly ninety kilometers. Blue water. Island-studded bays. Fishing boats at dawn.

Over two million people live along its shores.

And deep beneath its surface, Lake Kivu holds the same deadly combination that struck Nyos. Dissolved carbon dioxide. And something more. Billions of cubic meters of methane.

The deep water is dense, layered, and stable. For now.

Volcanoes stand just to the north. Earthquakes are common. Scientists have found evidence that lakes like this can overturn without warning.

So the gas is being taken out.

Floating platforms now pump methane from the depths, turning it into electricity for the region. A threat converted into power, slowly bleeding pressure from the bottom of the lake.

Sensors hang at different depths, tracking temperature and gas levels around the clock. Engineers return the treated water carefully, so the layers stay stable and nothing is disturbed too quickly.

Because if the deep water ever rose all at once, the gas cloud could be far larger than the one at Nyos. And the shores are far more crowded.

Every day, a little less danger. A little more light.

A lake that could one day suffocate a region now keeps its lights on.

## 4. VALLEY OF DEATH — Kamchatka, Russia

In Russia's far east, a different kind of air pools in the low ground.

On the Kamchatka Peninsula, tucked inside the Kronotsky Nature Reserve, a narrow valley sits at the foot of a volcano. Steaming vents. Bare stone. Patches of snow.

It looks peaceful.

Then you notice the bodies.

Foxes. Birds. Hares. Lying where they fell.

On still days, volcanic gases seep from the ground and settle in the hollow. Hydrogen sulfide. Carbon dioxide. Invisible, heavier than the surrounding air.

Small animals wander in and stop breathing.

Then the scavengers come to feed on them.

Wolverines. Eagles. Even brown bears, drawn in by the smell of an easy meal. They step into the same pocket of gas. And they fall beside what they came to eat.

The valley feeds on its own victims, one layer of hunger at a time.

Scientists who visit wear gas monitors and move quickly. Rangers watch the wind.

Locals have a simple name for it. The Valley of Death.

It is barely two kilometers long.

## 5. RÍO TINTO — Spain

Some rivers carry life. In southwestern Spain, one river carries something else.

The Río Tinto runs blood red through the hills of Andalusia. Orange banks. Purple pools. Water stained the color of rust.

The name simply means red river.

People have been mining these hills for around five thousand years. Copper. Silver. Gold. The ancient Tartessians dug here. Then the Romans. Then industrial companies that stripped entire mountains away.

The river runs through some of the richest metal deposits on Earth, and it has absorbed them.

Its water is rich in dissolved iron and heavy metals, with an acidity close to lemon juice. Fish cannot live in it. Most plants cannot grow along it.

And yet the river is not dead.

It is full of microbes. Organisms that feed on iron and sulfur, living without needing anything we would recognize as a habitat.

That is why space scientists came.

Researchers have studied the Río Tinto as a model for Mars. If life can thrive in water this toxic, it might survive in the ancient, acidic environments of another planet.

A river poisoned by Earth's metals may be teaching us how to find life beyond it.

## 6. THE BOILING RIVER — Peru

Deep in the Peruvian Amazon, a river steams under a canopy of rainforest.

At first, it looks like morning mist. Then you get closer.

The water is hot. Not warm. Hot enough to burn.

Shanay-Timpishka. In the local language, the name means boiled with the heat of the sun.

For more than six kilometers, the river runs through the jungle at temperatures that can climb above ninety degrees Celsius. Close to boiling point.

Small animals that slip from the banks are cooked before they can climb out. Frogs. Lizards. Birds. The current carries them downstream.

There is no volcano nearby.

The heat comes from deep underground. Water seeps down through faults in the rock, warms near the planet's interior, and is forced back up into the riverbed.

For generations, local healers have treated this place as sacred. A shamanic retreat sits on its bank. Some believe the river's spirit lives in the hottest stretch.

Outsiders long dismissed it as legend.

It wasn't. A river in the Amazon that can scald you to death.

## 7. ILHA DA QUEIMADA GRANDE — Brazil

But some of the deadliest places on Earth aren't defined by their water. They're defined by who lives there.

Thirty-three kilometers off the coast of São Paulo, a small island rises from the Atlantic. Green forest. Bare rock. A lighthouse at the top.

Ilha da Queimada Grande. Most people know it by another name.

Snake Island.

It is home to the golden lancehead. A pit viper found nowhere else on Earth.

When rising seas cut this island off from the mainland thousands of years ago, the snakes were stranded with no ground prey. So they adapted. They hunt in the trees and catch migrating birds.

Their venom evolved to work faster. A bird struck in the branches has to die before it can fly away.

Thousands of them live on an island smaller than fifty soccer fields.

Local stories tell of a lighthouse keeper and his family who were killed by snakes coming through the windows. No one can prove it. No one wants to test it.

Today, the Brazilian Navy bans the public from landing. Only scientists, with permits and medical support, are allowed ashore.

The lighthouse now runs itself.

No one stays on Snake Island.

## 8. NORTH SENTINEL ISLAND — India

In the Bay of Bengal, another island is closed to the outside world. Not by law alone. By its own people.

North Sentinel Island lies in India's Andaman Islands. A ring of white sand. A reef. Thick forest that hides everything inside.

The Sentinelese have lived here for thousands of years. No one knows exactly how many. A few dozen. Perhaps a few hundred.

They have made their position clear. Approach, and they defend the shore.

Arrows have been fired at boats. At aircraft. In 2004, after the Indian Ocean tsunami, a helicopter checking on the island was met by a man on the beach, aiming a bow at the sky.

Fishermen who drifted too close have been killed. Visitors who tried to land have not returned.

India now forbids anyone from coming within several kilometers.

The danger runs both ways.

The Sentinelese have lived in isolation for so long that a common cold could devastate them. A single visitor could carry an illness the island has never encountered.

So the island is protected from the world. And the world is kept from the island.

One of the last places on Earth that remains completely its own.

## 9. THE DARIÉN GAP — Panama and Colombia

The Pan-American Highway runs from Alaska to the southern tip of Argentina. Around thirty thousand kilometers of road.

Except for one place.

On the border of Panama and Colombia, the highway simply stops. And the jungle begins.

The Darién Gap.

Roughly a hundred kilometers of swamp, mountains, and rainforest that no road has ever crossed. Rivers rise without warning. Mud swallows boots to the knee. The heat and humidity never let up.

Venomous snakes. Biting insects. Steep, slippery ridges with no clear path.

For centuries, it was a barrier that almost no one tried to pass.

Then it became a route.

In recent years, hundreds of thousands of people have crossed it on foot, carrying children, trying to reach North America. In 2023 alone, more than half a million made the journey.

The crossing can take a week or more. Many are injured. Some are swept away. Some are never found.

A jungle so dense that engineers gave up on it.

And still, people walk through.

## 10. THE TROPICAL COAST — Northern Australia

Northern Australia's beaches look like paradise. Turquoise water. Empty sand. Warm sun almost every day of the year.

But at the entrance to many beaches, there's a bottle of vinegar.

Between roughly November and May, the coast enters stinger season. And the shallows fill with the box jellyfish.

It's nearly transparent. Pale. Easy to miss in clear water.

Its tentacles can trail up to three meters. Each is lined with millions of microscopic stinging cells. The venom attacks the heart and nervous system, and a heavy sting can kill an adult within minutes.

It is considered one of the most venomous animals in the ocean.

Swimmers wear full-body stinger suits. Some beaches set up enclosed nets. Vinegar stands ready to neutralize stinging cells that haven't fired yet.

And the jellyfish are only part of the problem.

In the rivers and estuaries along the same coast, saltwater crocodiles wait. The largest reptiles on Earth. Some longer than five meters.

They move between river and sea with ease.

A coastline built for postcards.

Where the water itself can kill you.

## 11. THE SUNDARBANS — India and Bangladesh

But crocodiles are not the only predators that hunt people at the water's edge.

Where the Ganges and Brahmaputra rivers meet the Bay of Bengal, the land dissolves into thousands of channels and islands.

The Sundarbans. The largest mangrove forest on Earth.

Around ten thousand square kilometers of tangled roots, mudflats, and tidal creeks, split between India and Bangladesh.

Twice a day, the tide floods the forest. Twice a day, it drains away. The ground is never quite solid. The water is never quite fresh.

And in this shifting maze lives the Bengal tiger.

The tigers here are unlike any others. They swim between islands. They drink brackish water. They move through the mangroves almost unseen.

And they hunt people.

Fishermen, crab catchers, and honey collectors work deep in the forest, because the forest is how they survive. Every season, some of them do not come home.

So they adapted.

Workers have worn masks on the backs of their heads, because tigers tend to attack from behind. A face looking backward can make a tiger hesitate.

In villages along the edge of the forest, there are communities known locally as tiger-widow villages. Entire streets where the men went into the mangroves and never came back.

Still, every morning, the boats go out.

The forest gives food, wood, and honey.

It also takes its share.

## 12. DEATH VALLEY — California, United States

Some places don't need predators. They have heat.

In eastern California, between two mountain ranges, a basin sinks below sea level.

Death Valley.

At Badwater Basin, the ground sits eighty-six meters below the ocean's surface. The lowest point in North America. A white sheet of salt cracked into endless polygons.

On July 10th, 1913, the air at Furnace Creek reached fifty-six point seven degrees Celsius. Still the hottest air temperature ever officially recorded on Earth.

The ground gets hotter. Close to ninety-four degrees has been measured on the surface. Hot enough to burn bare feet in seconds.

In summer, the nights barely cool. Some stay above forty degrees until dawn.

The mountains trap the heat. The air sinks, compresses, and warms even further. The basin bakes like an oven with the door shut.

Heat-related emergencies are routine here. Cars break down. Hikers misjudge distances. Water runs out faster than anyone expects.

And yet, in a dry lakebed called Racetrack Playa, heavy stones slide across the ground on their own, leaving long trails behind them.

For decades, no one could explain it. Then researchers finally caught it on camera. Thin sheets of winter ice, pushed by the wind, shove the rocks across the mud.

Even in Death Valley, there's a winter. It just doesn't last.

## 13. THE LUT DESERT — Iran

But the hottest ground on Earth isn't in America.

In southeastern Iran, the Lut Desert spreads across tens of thousands of square kilometers of sand, salt, and stone.

From space, it glows.

Satellites measuring land surface temperatures have recorded readings here above seventy degrees Celsius. Some of the highest ground temperatures ever detected anywhere on the planet.

Parts of the desert are covered in black volcanic rock that absorbs the sun and holds it.

There are almost no plants. Few animals. In its hottest corner, almost nothing grows at all.

But the wind is busy.

Over thousands of years, it has carved giant ridges called kaluts. Towering walls of sediment, some over a hundred meters high, lined up in rows like ruined city blocks.

From above, they look like the remains of a civilization.

They're not. They're the work of air, moving relentlessly in one direction.

The Lut is so extreme that it was almost never studied up close. Even today, much of it remains unexplored on foot.

A desert that scientists understand better from orbit than from the ground.

## 14. THE SKELETON COAST — Namibia

On Africa's southwestern edge, the desert meets the sea in one of the most hostile shorelines on Earth.

Namibia's Skeleton Coast.

Cold ocean water flows north from Antarctica. Hot desert air sits above it. Where they collide, dense fog rolls in from the sea and smothers the coastline.

For centuries, ships sailed blind into it.

Hidden sandbanks. Violent surf. Currents that dragged hulls onto the shore.

The wrecks are still here. Rusted frames. Broken bows. Scattered across the beaches for hundreds of kilometers.

Some now lie hundreds of meters inland. The desert has pushed the coastline outward, leaving ships stranded in the sand, far from the water they sank in.

The name comes from the bones. Whale skeletons from old whaling stations. And the remains of shipwrecked sailors who made it to shore, only to find no water, no shade, and no way out.

Portuguese sailors called it the Gates of Hell.

The San people of the interior had a different name. The land God made in anger.

Even now, the coast is mostly empty. Access is restricted. Lions, hyenas, and jackals patrol the beaches, scavenging seals at the water's edge.

A desert that ends in the ocean.

And an ocean that ends in the desert.

## 15. OYMYAKON — Russia

From the furnace to the freezer.

In northeastern Siberia, a village of roughly five hundred people endures the coldest winters of any permanently inhabited place on Earth.

Oymyakon.

In 1933, the temperature here dropped to minus sixty-seven point seven degrees Celsius.

In winter, minus fifty is normal.

At these temperatures, exposed skin can freeze in minutes. Eyelashes frost over. Breath crystallizes and falls.

Cars are kept running for hours or parked inside heated garages, because once an engine freezes, it may not start again until spring. Batteries fail. Fuel thickens. Metal becomes brittle.

Most homes rely on wood or coal stoves burning around the clock. Many still use outdoor toilets, because the ground is too frozen for plumbing.

The ground itself is permafrost. Frozen solid, year-round.

Burying the dead takes days. Fires must be lit on the ground to thaw each layer before it can be dug.

The only road in is the Kolyma Highway. Built in the Soviet era by prisoners of the Gulag. Thousands died during its construction.

Locals still call it the Road of Bones.

Children go to school until it drops below roughly minus fifty-two.

Then, finally, they stay home.

## 16. MOUNT WASHINGTON — New Hampshire, United States

But you don't need Siberia to find deadly cold.

In the northeastern United States, a mountain only 1,917 meters tall has some of the most violent weather on the planet.

Mount Washington.

It sits where several major storm tracks meet. Weather from the Atlantic, the Gulf of Mexico, and the Pacific Northwest all funnel toward it.

On April 12th, 1934, instruments at the summit recorded a wind gust of 372 kilometers per hour. For more than sixty years, it was the fastest wind ever measured on Earth.

Hurricane-force winds strike the summit on more than a hundred days a year.

Rime ice builds sideways on every surface, growing into the wind like white feathers.

The summit weather station looks like a bunker, chained and anchored to the rock.

The danger is that the mountain looks easy.

Hikers set out in mild sunshine from the base. Within hours, the summit is in whiteout. Visibility gone. Temperatures falling past freezing. Winds strong enough to knock people off their feet.

Since the 1800s, more than 160 people have died on its slopes.

The sign at the trailhead is blunt.

The area ahead has the worst weather in America. Many have died there from exposure, even in the summer.

## 17. CATATUMBO LIGHTNING — Venezuela

Further south, the sky itself becomes the danger.

In northwestern Venezuela, the Catatumbo River flows into Lake Maracaibo. And above the place where they meet, the night almost never stays dark.

Catatumbo lightning.

On well over a hundred nights a year, storms gather here after sunset and flash for hours without stopping.

Warm, humid air rises off the lake. Cold winds drop from the Andes. Where they collide, towering clouds form night after night, in almost exactly the same place.

Scientists have measured more lightning here per square kilometer than almost anywhere else on the planet. In the most active stretches, the sky can flash dozens of times a minute.

Often, there's barely any thunder. The storms are so far away that the light arrives without the sound.

For centuries, sailors used it to navigate. They called it the Lighthouse of Maracaibo.

But beneath it, people live in villages built on stilts over the water. Wooden houses. Open boats. Fishing nets drying in the wind.

When the storms begin, there is nowhere higher to hide.

They have learned to read the sky. To know when to come in from the lake. To wait it out.

A storm that returns almost every night. And never seems to run out of light.

## 18. SALTSTRAUMEN — Norway

On Norway's northern coast, the sea doesn't just move. It fights.

Near the town of Bodø, a narrow channel connects a vast fjord to the open ocean.

Every six hours, the tide turns. And roughly four hundred million cubic meters of seawater try to force their way through a strait barely 150 meters wide.

Saltstraumen.

The strongest tidal current on Earth.

Water rushes through at up to forty kilometers per hour. The surface twists. Bulges. Splits.

Then the whirlpools form.

Spinning funnels up to ten meters across and five meters deep, appearing and vanishing in seconds. Pulling down anything that drifts into them.

Small boats can be spun, swamped, and dragged under.

Fishermen have worked this channel for centuries, timing their trips to the slack tide, the short calm between surges. The current is so strong it stirs up nutrients and concentrates fish. The richest fishing grounds sit right beside the most dangerous water.

When the tide turns, the window closes.

And the sea pulls itself apart again.

## 19. THE DRAKE PASSAGE AND CAPE HORN — South America and Antarctica

Further south than any other continent but one, South America breaks into islands and ends in a black cliff.

Cape Horn.

Beyond it lies the Drake Passage. Around eight hundred kilometers of open ocean between South America and Antarctica.

Here, nothing stops the wind.

The Southern Ocean circles the globe with no landmass to block it. Westerly winds build without interruption. Swells grow for thousands of kilometers before they arrive.

Waves over ten meters are common. Rogue waves can rise far higher.

For centuries, this was the only sea route between the Atlantic and the Pacific. Before the Panama Canal, every ship heading west had to round the Horn.

Hundreds of them never made it.

Masts snapped. Hulls split. Sailors were washed overboard into water just above freezing.

The waters around Cape Horn became known as a graveyard of ships. Thousands of sailors are believed to lie on the seabed here.

Today, expedition ships still cross the Drake to reach Antarctica. Passengers brace for two days of violent seas.

Sometimes, the passage is calm. Sailors call it the Drake Lake.

More often, they call it the Drake Shake.

## 20. SABLE ISLAND — Canada

In the North Atlantic, another graveyard lies hidden in the fog.

Three hundred kilometers off the coast of Nova Scotia, a thin crescent of sand curves across the ocean.

Sable Island.

No trees. No rock. Just dunes, grass, and long empty beaches.

From the deck of a ship, it is almost invisible. Low, pale, and wrapped in fog for much of the year.

Shifting sandbars extend far beneath the surface. Currents from the north and south collide here. Storms sweep through with little warning.

More than 350 shipwrecks have been recorded around the island.

Sailors called it the Graveyard of the Atlantic.

The sand keeps moving. Wrecks are buried, uncovered, and buried again.

And on the dunes, wild horses roam freely. Around five hundred of them. No one feeds them. No one rides them. They have survived here on their own for over two centuries.

Today, the island is a protected national park. Only a handful of people live here, mostly researchers and weather staff.

Every visit depends on the fog lifting.

Sometimes, it never does.

## 21. THE BLUE HOLE — Dahab, Egypt

On the Red Sea coast of Egypt, a few steps from the shore, the reef suddenly drops away into dark blue.

The Blue Hole of Dahab.

From the surface, it's a perfect circle. Calm. Clear. Surrounded by coral and bright tropical fish.

It's one of the most famous dive sites in the world.

And one of the deadliest.

The hole plunges more than a hundred meters down. At around fifty-five meters, a tunnel called the Arch connects it to the open sea.

That tunnel is the problem.

From inside the hole, light pours through the Arch, making it look closer and shorter than it is. Divers swim toward it.

At that depth, nitrogen begins to affect the brain. Judgment fades. Divers can feel calm, even confident, while their air runs out faster than they realize.

Some never find their way back up.

Estimates of the number of divers who have died here run well over a hundred.

On the rocks along the shore, memorial plaques carry their names.

Every day, divers still enter the water, admiring the reef from the safe upper edge.

Below them, the blue keeps going.

## 22. THE CHALLENGER DEEP — Pacific Ocean

But no hole on Earth compares to the deepest one.

In the western Pacific, south of Guam, the ocean floor plunges into the Mariana Trench. And at its southern end lies the Challenger Deep.

Nearly eleven kilometers below the surface.

If Mount Everest were placed at the bottom, its summit would still sit more than two kilometers underwater.

The pressure here is over a thousand times greater than at sea level. Like the weight of a large truck pressing down on every square inch.

There is no sunlight. The water is just above freezing. Any ordinary vessel would be crushed instantly.

In 1960, two men descended in a steel bathyscaphe called the Trieste. On the way down, they heard a loud crack. One of the outer windows had fractured.

They kept going.

They reached the bottom and spent about twenty minutes in the deepest place on the planet.

For more than fifty years, no one returned.

For decades, more people had walked on the Moon than had reached the Challenger Deep.

And yet, life is here.

Pale, shrimp-like creatures scavenge the seafloor. Single-celled organisms build delicate shells in the mud.

They survive under pressure that would flatten a submarine.

## 23. VERYOVKINA CAVE — Abkhazia, Georgia

The ocean has its depths. So does the land.

In the mountains above the Black Sea, on the Arabika Massif, a narrow opening leads into the deepest known cave on Earth.

Veryovkina.

It plunges more than two thousand two hundred meters into the mountain. Deeper than five Empire State Buildings stacked end to end.

Reaching the bottom takes days.

Explorers descend on ropes through vertical shafts. Squeeze through cracks barely wider than a body. Swim through freezing pools in total darkness.

They camp underground for weeks. Sleeping in tents pitched on ledges. Cooking on small stoves. Never seeing daylight.

And the cave is alive with water.

Rain and snowmelt on the surface can trigger floods far below. Water levels can rise fast, filling passages that were dry hours before.

Expeditions have been forced to race upward, climbing for their lives as floodwater surged behind them.

At the bottom lies a dark sump. A flooded passage leading even deeper, into places no human has ever seen.

The deepest point we know of is only the deepest point we have reached.

## 24. THE YUNGAS ROAD — Bolivia

High in the Bolivian Andes, a dirt road drops from snowy peaks into the rainforest.

Over around sixty kilometers, it descends more than three thousand meters. From cold mountain air to tropical heat in a single journey.

The Yungas Road.

In places, it's barely wider than a single vehicle. No guardrails. Cliffs drop hundreds of meters straight down.

Waterfalls pour across the road. Mud turns the surface to grease. Fog rolls up from the valleys and erases the edge.

For decades, it was the main route between La Paz and the Amazon lowlands. Buses, trucks, and cars all shared it.

When two vehicles met, one had to reverse along the cliff edge to let the other pass.

Crosses line the bends. Each one marks a fall.

It became known around the world by a single name. Death Road.

A newer highway now carries most of the traffic. The old road is quieter.

But not empty.

Today, thousands of tourists ride it on mountain bikes every year, chasing the thrill of the most dangerous road on Earth.

The cliffs haven't moved.

## 25. LA RINCONADA — Peru

Some people live closer to the sky than anywhere else on Earth.

In the Peruvian Andes, at over 5,000 meters above sea level, a town clings to the slopes beneath a glacier.

La Rinconada. The highest permanent settlement in the world.

Tens of thousands of people live here. The air holds roughly half the oxygen found at sea level. Every flight of stairs feels like a sprint.

Temperatures drop below freezing almost every night. There's no reliable running water. No sewage system. Waste and mercury drain from the mines into the ice and soil.

So why live here?

Gold.

Beneath the glacier, the mountain is laced with it. Miners crawl into tunnels with picks and dynamite, digging for a fortune that rarely comes.

Many work under a system called cachorreo. For thirty days, they labor without pay. On the final day, they keep whatever ore they can carry out.

It might be worthless. It might change their lives.

Most stay for years, chasing the next lucky day.

A town built on gold.

At an altitude where the human body slowly begins to fail.

## 26. ANNAPURNA — Nepal

In central Nepal, a wall of ice and rock towers above the clouds.

Annapurna I. 8,091 meters high.

In 1950, it became the first mountain over eight thousand meters ever climbed by humans. Before Everest. Before K2.

And for decades, it was the deadliest.

For many years, roughly one climber died for every three who reached the summit.

Not because it is the tallest. It isn't.

Because it is unstable.

Huge overhanging seracs, cliffs of ice the size of buildings, hang above the climbing routes. They collapse without warning. Avalanches sweep across the south face. Storms move in quickly and stay.

Climbers can do everything right and still be buried.

Even far below the summit, the region can turn lethal. In October 2014, a sudden blizzard struck the trekking routes around the Annapurna range. Dozens of people died in snow they never expected.

Some still come to climb it.

Most mountaineers measure success by reaching the top.

On Annapurna, success is coming back down.

## 27. THE SIACHEN GLACIER — Karakoram

But some people don't climb these heights by choice. They are stationed there.

In the eastern Karakoram, between India and Pakistan, a river of ice stretches for around seventy kilometers through some of the highest mountains on Earth.

The Siachen Glacier.

Since 1984, soldiers from both countries have held positions here, some at more than six thousand meters above sea level.

It's often called the highest battlefield in the world.

But the greatest enemy here isn't across the line.

It's the mountain.

Temperatures can drop below minus fifty. Winds tear across the ridges. Avalanches bury entire posts. Crevasses open beneath the snow without warning.

At this altitude, the body struggles to function. Sleep becomes difficult. Wounds heal slowly. Frostbite can take fingers and toes in hours.

Over the decades, far more soldiers have died here from weather, altitude, and avalanches than from combat.

Supplies arrive by helicopter, flying at the very limits of what their engines can handle in such thin air. Where helicopters can't land, loads are dropped onto the ice.

Soldiers rotate out after a few months. No one is meant to stay for long.

A glacier no one lives on.

And yet, it's never left empty.

## 28. MOUNT RAINIER — Washington, United States

From ice to fire.

Southeast of Seattle, a snow-capped mountain dominates the skyline. On clear days, it's visible from almost everywhere in the region.

Mount Rainier. 4,392 meters high.

It looks like a postcard.

It's actually an active volcano. And one of the most dangerous in the United States.

The real threat isn't lava. It's mud.

Rainier is covered in more glacial ice than any other peak in the lower forty-eight states. If an eruption, or even a large landslide, melts and dislodges that ice, it can mix with loose rock and soil.

The result is a lahar. A flowing river of mud and debris, moving with the consistency of wet concrete.

Around fifty-six hundred years ago, a lahar from Rainier traveled all the way to where Puget Sound now lies.

Today, tens of thousands of people live on top of old lahar deposits. Entire towns sit in the valleys those flows once filled.

In some of them, warning sirens stand along the roads.

Schoolchildren practice evacuation drills, walking to higher ground on a timer.

Above them, the mountain sits quietly.

## 29. SAKURAJIMA — Japan

But in southern Japan, a city of six hundred thousand people lives with a volcano across the bay.

Kagoshima.

And four kilometers away, Sakurajima.

It erupts constantly. Small explosions. Ash plumes rising thousands of meters. In some years, hundreds of eruptions.

For people here, this is weather.

Ash forecasts are part of the daily news, telling residents which way the wind will carry it. Umbrellas are used against falling ash as often as rain.

The city hands out special bags so people can collect ash from their homes and leave it at designated points for removal.

On the volcano's lower slopes, villages remain. Concrete shelters line the roads, built so residents can take cover if rocks come raining down.

In 1914, a massive eruption poured out so much lava that it filled the strait and joined what was once an island to the mainland.

Thousands of residents evacuated. Many moved away for good.

But others came back.

The soil is rich. The views are extraordinary. The hot springs are warm year-round.

And the mountain is never quiet for long.

## 30. MOUNT NYIRAGONGO — Democratic Republic of the Congo

In the eastern Congo, a volcano rises above the city of Goma.

Mount Nyiragongo.

At its summit, a crater holds one of the largest lava lakes on Earth. A churning pool of molten rock, glowing red at night, bubbling and cracking like a living surface.

Most lava moves slowly. You can walk away from it.

Not here.

Nyiragongo's lava is unusually fluid, low in silica, running almost like water. In 1977, it raced down the mountain at up to sixty kilometers per hour. Some of the fastest-moving lava ever recorded.

In 2002, the volcano erupted again. Lava flowed straight into Goma. Through houses. Through markets. Across the airport runway.

Hundreds of thousands of people fled across the border.

When they returned, much of the city had been buried under black rock.

So they rebuilt. On top of it.

Today, over a million people live in Goma. Many of them on hardened lava from past eruptions.

In 2021, Nyiragongo erupted once more, sending another river of fire toward the city.

The lava stopped at the city's edge.

This time.

## 31. ANAK KRAKATAU — Indonesia

In the strait between Java and Sumatra, a volcanic island rises from the sea where an older giant once stood.

In 1883, Krakatoa exploded with such force that the sound was heard nearly five thousand kilometers away. One of the loudest sounds in recorded history.

Tsunamis tore across the coasts. Tens of thousands of people died.

The island was gone.

Then, in 1927, smoke appeared above the water.

A new volcano had begun to grow from the drowned crater.

Anak Krakatau. Child of Krakatoa.

For decades, it rose. Erupting. Building. Growing higher year after year.

Scientists watched it closely. Tourists came to see it. Fishermen worked around it.

Then, on the night of December 22nd, 2018, part of the volcano collapsed into the sea.

There was almost no warning.

The landslide pushed a wall of water outward. Waves struck the shores of Java and Sumatra in the dark, sweeping through beaches, hotels, and villages.

More than four hundred people died.

The volcano had lost much of its height in a single night.

It's been rebuilding ever since.

The child of Krakatoa is growing up.

## 32. CENTRALIA — Pennsylvania, United States

Nature builds many deadly places.

But some of the most dangerous were made by us.

In eastern Pennsylvania, a small town sits almost empty.

Centralia.

In 1962, a fire started in the town's garbage dump. It spread into an exposed seam of coal.

Then it went underground.

The coal beneath Centralia runs through miles of old mine tunnels. The fire followed them.

Over the following decades, the ground grew hot. Smoke leaked from cracks in roads and yards. Carbon monoxide seeped into basements. The earth opened in sinkholes.

Residents were relocated. Houses were demolished, one after another.

A town of over a thousand people was reduced to a handful.

Roads cracked and buckled. A stretch of highway was abandoned and covered in graffiti before being buried under dirt.

The fire is still burning.

Some estimates say it could continue for another two hundred and fifty years.

Above it, trees grow over empty streets.

And on cold days, steam still rises from the ground.

## 33. LAKE PEIGNEUR — Louisiana, United States

Sometimes, the danger we create takes decades to unfold.

Sometimes, it takes a single morning.

In southern Louisiana, Lake Peigneur was once a shallow freshwater lake, only about three meters deep. Fishermen worked it. A botanical garden sat on its shore.

Beneath it lay a salt mine. A maze of huge tunnels carved deep into an underground dome of salt.

On November 20th, 1980, an oil rig on the lake was drilling into the lakebed.

It drilled into the mine.

Water began pouring through the hole. Then the hole widened.

Within hours, the entire lake was draining into the earth.

A giant whirlpool formed on the surface. It swallowed the drilling rig. Then a tugboat. Then a string of barges, one by one, pulled down into the vortex.

Miners raced through the tunnels below as water flooded in, escaping just in time.

The canal that connected the lake to the Gulf reversed direction. For a few days, it poured into the empty basin as a waterfall dozens of meters high.

When it was over, the shallow freshwater lake was gone.

In its place, a deep saltwater lake.

Remarkably, no one died.

Days later, nine of the sunken barges popped back up to the surface like corks.

## 34. WITTENOOM — Western Australia

In the red gorges of Western Australia, a town was erased from the map.

Wittenoom.

In the mid-twentieth century, it was a busy mining town. Workers came from all over the world to dig blue asbestos from the rock.

The dust was everywhere. In the mine. On the roads. In the playground. Children played in it.

No one fully understood what it would do.

Asbestos fibers lodge in the lungs. Decades later, they cause cancers and diseases that cannot be cured.

The mine closed in 1966. But the damage was already done.

Thousands of former workers and residents have since died from asbestos-related illness.

The dust stayed behind.

The government withdrew services. Then removed the town from official maps and road signs. Visitors were warned to stay away.

A few residents refused to leave for years.

Eventually, they were gone too.

Today, the buildings are being demolished.

The gorges are still beautiful.

The dust is still there.

## 35. GRUINARD ISLAND — Scotland

Off the northwest coast of Scotland, a small grassy island sits quietly in a sheltered bay.

Gruinard Island.

In 1942, during the Second World War, British scientists brought sheep here.

Then they released anthrax.

They were testing biological weapons. The sheep died within days.

The tests worked. Too well.

Anthrax spores can survive in soil for decades. The island was contaminated so thoroughly that it was quarantined.

For nearly fifty years, no one was allowed to land.

Warning signs stood on the shore. The grass grew. The island waited.

Fishermen steered clear of it. Locals on the mainland could see it from their windows, green and silent, barely a kilometer away.

In 1981, a group protesting the government's inaction secretly landed on the island and collected soil. They left samples outside military research buildings to force the issue.

The pressure worked.

In the 1980s, workers in protective suits finally returned. They soaked the soil with formaldehyde and seawater and removed the most contaminated earth.

In 1990, the island was declared safe.

Sheep graze there again today.

But for half a century, a small island in Scotland was one of the most dangerous places in the world.

## 36. LAKE KARACHAY — Russia

In Russia's southern Ural Mountains, there was once a small, ordinary lake.

Then it became a dumping ground.

Lake Karachay sits near the Mayak nuclear facility, one of the Soviet Union's first plutonium plants.

Starting in the 1950s, radioactive waste was poured directly into the lake.

Year after year.

By 1990, it was considered the most radioactively polluted place on Earth. Researchers estimated that standing on its shore for about an hour could deliver a lethal dose of radiation.

In 1967, a drought exposed the lakebed. The wind picked up radioactive dust and carried it across the surrounding region.

Hundreds of thousands of people were exposed.

The lake had to disappear.

Over the following decades, engineers filled it with concrete blocks, rock, and soil. Layer by layer. Sealing it from the air.

The work was finished in 2015.

Today, from above, it looks like an empty field.

What lies beneath has not gone anywhere.

## 37. BIKINI ATOLL — Marshall Islands

In the middle of the Pacific, a ring of coral islands encircles a turquoise lagoon.

White sand. Palm trees. Clear water full of fish.

Bikini Atoll.

It looks like the most peaceful place on Earth.

In 1946, the United States asked the 167 people who lived here to leave. Temporarily, they were told.

Over the next twelve years, twenty-three nuclear weapons were detonated at Bikini.

In 1954, a test called Castle Bravo released around a thousand times the energy of the bomb dropped on Hiroshima. Far more than its designers expected.

Radioactive fallout drifted across nearby islands and onto a Japanese fishing boat.

Entire islands were vaporized. Craters were blasted into the reef.

In the 1970s, some families were allowed to return. But tests found radioactive material building up in their bodies from the local food. They were evacuated again.

Today, the islands remain largely empty.

And beneath the lagoon, something unexpected happened.

Coral has grown back over the craters. Sharks patrol the reefs. Warships sunk in the tests now rest on the seabed, covered in marine life.

The ocean has recovered.

The people still have not returned home.

## 38. THE ARAL SEA — Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan

On the border of Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, there was once the fourth-largest lake on Earth.

The Aral Sea.

Fishing towns lined its shores. Ships worked its waters. It supplied fish to much of the Soviet Union.

Then, in the 1960s, the rivers that fed it were diverted to irrigate cotton fields.

The sea began to shrink.

By the 2010s, most of it had vanished.

What remains is desert. A new one. The Aralkum.

Rusted ships sit in the sand, many kilometers from any water. In the town of Moynaq, the harbor now looks out on dunes.

The exposed seabed is laced with salt, pesticides, and fertilizer residue. Storms lift it into toxic dust clouds that sweep across the region. Respiratory illnesses rose. Crops failed.

And in the middle of the old sea, an island once housed a secret Soviet biological weapons lab. As the water retreated, the island joined the mainland.

Recovery has begun in the north, where a dam has helped part of the sea return.

But most of the Aral is gone.

A sea that was not destroyed by nature.

Just drained.

## EPILOGUE

Every one of these places tells the same story in a different language.

Some were shaped by fire rising from below. Some by poison in the air. Some by ice, wind, or water that does not forgive mistakes.

And some, we built ourselves.

Lakes that breathe out death. Rivers that boil. Islands no one can visit. Towns that burn, freeze, or vanish from the map.

They remind us how thin the margin really is. A few degrees of heat. A few meters of depth. A few minutes without breathable air.

Most of the planet feels safe because we live in its gentlest corners. Step outside them, and the rules change quickly.

They are warnings.

But they are also proof.

Proof that flamingos can raise chicks in caustic water. That people can rebuild on the lava that buried their homes. That a forest can grow over an abandoned street, and a sea can begin to return.

The Earth is not built for our comfort.

It never was.

And still, we keep finding ways to stand at the edge of its most dangerous places.

To look closer.

To understand.

And sometimes, to call them home.
